## Kp and the G scale

**Kp** measures how disturbed Earth's magnetic field has been over a 3-hour period, from 0 (quiet) to 9 (extreme), worked out from magnetometers around the world. The higher it is, the further from the pole the aurora reaches. NOAA's storm scale starts at Kp 5:

| Kp | NOAA scale | Name | About how often |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | G1 | Minor | 900 days per cycle |
| 6 | G2 | Moderate | 360 days per cycle |
| 7 | G3 | Strong | 130 days per cycle |
| 8 | G4 | Severe | 60 days per cycle |
| 9 | G5 | Extreme | 4 days per cycle |

A cycle is the Sun's roughly 11-year activity cycle. Kp is a global average, so what it means for you depends on where you are: see [Magnetic latitude](/support/science/magnetic-latitude). Polar Lights works out the Kp your place **Needs** from your magnetic latitude.

## Bz

Bz is the north–south part of the magnetic field carried by the solar wind, in nanotesla (nT). It's the main switch for the aurora:

- **South (negative):** the solar wind's field links up with Earth's and lets energy in. Around −5 nT for a while helps; −10 nT or lower for 15 minutes or more often brings strong aurora.
- **North (positive):** little energy gets in, even if the wind is fast.

Bz can swing back and forth within minutes, which is why aurora can flare up and fade quickly.

## Solar wind speed

Typical wind is 300 to 400 km/s. Above about 500 km/s it carries more energy; 600 to 700 km/s or more is fast wind from a coronal hole or a CME. A sudden step up together with a jump in density is a **shock**, often the leading edge of a CME.

## Density

How many protons there are in each cubic centimetre of solar wind (p/cm³), typically 2 to 10. Denser wind pushes harder on Earth's field, but density on its own does little for the aurora. It matters most when Bz is south.

## Hemispheric power

NOAA's estimate of how much energy the aurora is putting into the upper atmosphere over a whole hemisphere, in gigawatts (GW):

- below about 20 GW, quiet aurora far to the north;
- around 30 to 50 GW, usually visible at high latitudes;
- above about 50 GW, bright aurora spreading towards lower latitudes.

A rise of tens of gigawatts within an hour often marks a substorm.
