Aurora forecasting has improved a lot, but it still can't promise a display. Knowing the limits helps you decide when to go out.

## How far ahead

| Range | Based on | How reliable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Next 30 to 90 minutes | Solar wind measured at L1, OVATION | The most reliable: the wind that will drive the aurora has already been measured. |
| Next 1 to 3 days | NOAA's Kp forecast, CME models | Good for timing, uncertain for strength. |
| Next 27 days | Recurring coronal holes | A guide to likely active periods, not specific nights. |

## Why CMEs are hard

Forecasters can see a CME leave the Sun and estimate when it will arrive, typically to within several hours either way. What they can't see in advance is the direction of its magnetic field. A CME whose field points south can bring a big storm; one pointing north may bring very little. That's only known when it passes the spacecraft at L1, less than an hour before it reaches Earth.

## Why short-term changes happen

Bz can swing between north and south within minutes, and substorms release energy in sudden bursts. A quiet sky can light up in minutes, and a bright display can fade just as fast. That's what the short-range alerts are for.

## Local conditions

Even a perfect space forecast can be spoiled by cloud, moonlight, light pollution or twilight. Polar Lights combines these with the aurora forecast in the **Tonight** score, but cloud forecasts carry their own errors; the satellite check in **Your sky** helps catch them.

## Making the most of it

- Check the **Tonight** card for the best window, and look again before you go.
- Turn on alerts so you hear when things change. See [Set up aurora alerts](/support/start/set-up-alerts).
- Use **Best nights ahead** for planning trips, not for promises.
- When in doubt, go out: OVATION tends to be cautious, and aurora is often seen somewhat further from the pole than its line suggests.
