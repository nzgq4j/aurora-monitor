The auroral oval is centred on Earth's **magnetic** pole, not the geographic North Pole. The northern geomagnetic pole, which the oval circles, lies at about 80.8°N, 72.6°W, between Ellesmere Island in the Canadian Arctic and northern Greenland: about 1,000 km from the geographic pole. So what matters for the aurora is your **magnetic latitude**: how far you are from the magnetic pole, measured in degrees like ordinary latitude.

## What this means

- **North America is closer to the magnetic pole** than Europe at the same map latitude. Edmonton and Hamburg are both at about 53.5°N, but Edmonton's magnetic latitude is about 60° and Hamburg's about 54°. Polar Lights reckons Edmonton needs about Kp 2 for aurora low on the horizon, and Hamburg about Kp 5.
- **Siberia and northern Scandinavia** are further from the magnetic pole than their latitude suggests, so the oval sits further north there.
- **In the south**, the geomagnetic pole is offset towards Australia. Hobart, at about 43°S, is at about 50° magnetic latitude, which is why Tasmania and southern New Zealand see the southern lights during strong storms.

## How Polar Lights uses it

The **Your sky** card shows your magnetic latitude (**Mag. lat**) and, under **Needs**, the Kp at which aurora is usually seen from your place. Places right under the auroral zone can see aurora even at Kp 0. Further from the pole, every 2° of magnetic latitude adds roughly one point of Kp.

## It changes

Earth's magnetic field shifts slowly. The north magnetic pole, where a compass needle points straight down, has moved from northern Canada towards Siberia over the past century; the geomagnetic pole that the oval follows moves much less. Polar Lights works out magnetic latitude from the 2025 International Geomagnetic Reference Field (IGRF), using its dipole: a good approximation for aurora forecasting, though local differences of a degree or so are possible.
