The Sun tab shows the Sun as several spacecraft see it. Each view picks out a different temperature or layer. Å (ångström) is a unit of wavelength: one tenth of a nanometre.

## The quick look

- **AIA 211·193·171:** three ultraviolet channels (about 0.6 to 2 million °C) combined. Coronal holes stand out as dark areas and active regions as bright knots: the quickest view of what could affect Earth over the next few days.
- **SUVI map:** NOAA's map from GOES-19 that sorts the Sun into quiet Sun, active regions, coronal holes, filaments, prominences and flares, each in its own colour.

## Coronal holes and the corona

- **AIA 193 Å:** iron at about 1.5 million °C, showing the corona. Coronal holes are the large dark areas; when one faces Earth, fast wind usually arrives 2 to 4 days later.
- **AIA 211 Å:** iron at about 2 million °C. Active regions glow and coronal holes show with sharper edges than at 193 Å.
- **SUVI 195 Å** and **SUVI 284 Å:** similar views from NOAA's GOES-19, with a wider field that shows loops and eruptions beyond the edge of the Sun. 284 Å shows coronal holes with strong contrast.
- **AIA 171 Å:** the corona at about 600,000 °C. Bright arcs are coronal loops above active regions; tangled loop systems mark where flares are most likely.

## Active regions and flares

- **AIA 131 Å:** the hottest material, about 10 million °C. A flare shows as a sudden, very bright spot.
- **AIA 94 Å:** about 6 million °C, the hot cores of active regions. Regions that stay bright here are the ones to watch.
- **AIA 335 Å:** about 2.5 million °C. Dimmer and grainier, but shows the heating above complex sunspot groups.
- **AIA 1600 Å:** the top of the surface and just above. During a flare, bright ribbons appear where its energy reaches the lower atmosphere.
- **AIA 1700 Å:** the surface itself; bright patches around sunspots trace the magnetic field.

## Sunspots and magnetic field

- **HMI:** the Sun in visible light, as through a solar filter. Sunspots are darker, cooler patches where strong magnetic fields hold back heat. Each group is labelled with its NOAA region number.
- **HMI magnetogram:** the magnetic field at the surface, with the two polarities in contrasting colours. Tightly packed opposite polarities store the most energy and produce most large flares.

## Prominences and filaments

- **AIA 304 Å:** helium at about 50,000 °C, low in the atmosphere. Prominences are clouds of cooler gas above the edge of the Sun; seen against the disc they're dark threads called filaments. An erupting filament can launch a CME.

## CMEs: the coronagraphs

A coronagraph blocks the bright disc to show the faint corona around it, where CMEs appear as expanding bright clouds. A ring spreading all around the disc, a **halo CME**, is heading towards Earth or directly away from it.

- **LASCO C2:** SOHO's view out to about six times the Sun's radius.
- **LASCO C3:** a much wider view, out to about 30 times the radius. Bright dots are stars and planets.
- **CCOR-1:** NOAA's coronagraph on GOES-19, built for space weather forecasting.
- **CCOR-2:** NOAA's coronagraph on SOLAR-1 (formerly SWFO-L1), 1.5 million km towards the Sun.
- **CCOR-1 Δ** and **CCOR-2 Δ:** each image minus the one before, so only changes show. A CME appears as a bright front with darkness behind it: the easiest way to spot one.

Images arrive on each spacecraft's own schedule. See [Why are some Sun images an hour old?](/support/faq/why-are-sun-images-old)
